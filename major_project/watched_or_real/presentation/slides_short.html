Key Experiment 1: Activation Steering

Aim:

E1 showed that the model has a “Real vs. Simulated” direction inside it. Here we asked: if we push the model along this direction, does its behaviour change?

How we did?

  • Model: Ministral-8B. Dataset: 520 harmful requests (AdvBench).
  • The requests had no real/simulated sentence; they looked like ordinary test questions.
  • While the model was answering, we added the direction to its internal activations:

$h' = h + \alpha\, g\, \hat d_R$

  • $\alpha$ negative → push toward Simulated; $\alpha$ positive → push toward Real.
  • As a control, we also added random directions of the same size.

Results

ConditionHarmful compliance
No push5.6%
Push toward Simulated30.4%
Push toward Real0.6%
Random push6–8%

What this means:

  1. Making the model “think” the situation is simulated made it about 5× more willing to help with harmful requests.
  2. Making it “think” the situation is real made it refuse almost everything.
  3. Random pushes did almost nothing, so the effect comes from this specific direction.
Harmful compliance under each push (Ministral-8B, 520 requests). Table: HarmBench judge; chart: Qwen3 judge. Both show the same pattern.

Key Experiment 2: Attention-Head Knockout

Aim:

Experiment 1 showed the direction changes behaviour. Here we asked: which parts of the model create this signal?

How we did?

  • A model has hundreds of small units called attention heads. For each one, we measured how much it writes the Real-vs-Simulated signal.
  • We took the top 10 heads and switched them off (set their output to zero):

$o'_{\text{head}} = 0$

  • We then measured how much of the gap between “simulated” and “real” behaviour disappeared.
  • As a control, we also switched off 10 random heads.

Results

ModelTop-10 heads off10 random heads off
Ministral-8B78% of gap removedup to 39%
Llama-3.1-8B81% of gap removed0–44%*

*one random set also removed the gap; it contained one of the top-10 heads.

What this means:

  1. Just 10 attention heads create most of the Real-vs-Simulated signal.
  2. Switching them off removes most of the behaviour difference; random heads do much less.
  3. Setting these heads to their “real” state makes a simulated scenario behave like a real one (Llama: 67.4% → 44.6%, real = 46.4%).
Framing gap after switching off the top-10 heads (orange) vs random heads (grey).

Conclusion

  1. Language models keep two separate signals inside: “Am I being watched?” and “Are the consequences real?” (E1).
  2. The “real” signal controls how cautious the model is: pushing it changes harmful compliance from 0.6% to 30.4%.
  3. About 10 attention heads create this signal; switching them off removes about 80% of the effect.

Why it matters

AI safety tests assume models behave the same whether or not they think the situation is real. Our results show this belief can be read, and controlled, from inside the model.
